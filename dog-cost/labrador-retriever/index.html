How Much Does a Labrador Retriever Cost?

America's most popular breed is a friendly, high-energy, medium-large dog — and a solidly mid-range bill. Here's the full lifetime picture.

What drives a Lab's vet bills

Hip and elbow dysplasia are the breed's signature problem — malformed joints that lead to arthritis (see our arthritis guide). Mild cases are managed with medication and weight control; severe cases can mean $3,500–$7,000 hip-replacement surgery. Reputable breeders screen hips and elbows (OFA/PennHIP) — one more reason the breeder's price premium can pay for itself.

Exercise-induced collapse (EIC) causes episodes of weakness or collapse after intense exercise in genetically susceptible Labs. There's a DNA test for it; affected dogs simply need their exercise moderated — not expensive, but essential to know about.

The other Lab tax is appetite: this is the breed most likely to eat something it shouldn't. Budget for at least one foreign-body scare or plan your training accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

Are Labrador Retrievers expensive to own?

Mid-range for a large breed: about $3,920 the first year and $2,900 annually after, or roughly $32,920 over an 11-year life on a Standard care plan. They're cheaper than giant breeds but pricier than small dogs, mostly because everything — food, medication doses, surgery — scales with their 55–80 lb size.

What health problems should Lab owners budget for?

Hip and elbow dysplasia (and the arthritis that follows), cruciate-ligament injuries from their athletic lifestyle, and dietary indiscretion — Labs eat things. An emergency fund of $5,000 covers most single incidents.
