Arthritis in Dogs: Symptoms, Treatment Options & What It Costs

Senior Dog Care series · Updated October 2026 · Educational content, not veterinary advice

If your once-spry dog now groans getting up in the morning, hesitates at the stairs, or lags behind on walks they used to lead, you're probably looking at the most common diagnosis in senior dogs: osteoarthritis, the degenerative joint disease most of us just call arthritis. Studies estimate it affects up to 80% of dogs over age 8 to some degree — and it's one of the most manageable chronic conditions in veterinary medicine, if it's caught and treated consistently.

This guide walks through what arthritis is, how to spot it early, what your vet will actually do about it, what each option costs, and how to make home life easier for a stiff dog. It is educational — not a substitute for your veterinarian, who needs to see your dog before any of this applies.

What arthritis actually is

Healthy joints are engineering marvels: two bone ends capped with slick cartilage, bathed in lubricating joint fluid, held stable by ligaments. In osteoarthritis, the cartilage gradually breaks down and the joint becomes inflamed. The body tries to compensate by growing extra bone around the joint (osteophytes, or bone spurs), which makes the joint stiffer and more painful — a slow feedback loop.

Two paths lead there. Primary osteoarthritis is simple wear and tear with age — the reason it shadows senior dogs. Secondary osteoarthritis follows an earlier joint problem: hip or elbow dysplasia, a cruciate-ligament tear, a fracture that healed imperfectly, or chronic obesity overloading the joints. Large and giant breeds are overrepresented because of their weight and their higher rates of dysplasia, but small dogs get arthritis too — they just tend to get it later.

The key fact that surprises most owners: arthritis can't be cured, but it can be managed very well. Dogs don't need perfect joints; they need pain controlled, weight controlled, and muscles kept strong enough to support the joints they have. Dogs on a consistent management plan often regain most of their quality of life.

Symptoms: early signs vs. advanced signs

Dogs are stoic. By the time most owners notice limping, the disease has usually been progressing quietly for months. Early signs are behavioral and easy to dismiss as "just getting old":

  • Stiffness after rest — slow to rise in the morning or after naps, then "walks it off" within minutes. This is the single most classic early sign.
  • Reluctance — hesitating at stairs, refusing to jump into the car, or choosing the elevator route on walks.
  • Shorter, slower walks — lagging behind, sitting down mid-walk, or tiring faster than last year.
  • Subtle behavior changes — irritability when touched near hips or elbows, less interest in play, sleeping more, licking or chewing at a particular joint.
  • Muscle loss — thinning thighs or shoulders on the affected side, because the dog is offloading weight.

Advanced signs are harder to miss: persistent limping, difficulty standing without assistance, crying out on certain movements, and in severe cases, reluctance to walk at all. If you recognize the early list, don't wait for the advanced list — earlier treatment preserves more joint function, and the exam is the same either way.

How vets diagnose it

Diagnosis starts with a hands-on orthopedic exam: the vet watches your dog walk, then flexes and extends each joint feeling for pain responses, reduced range of motion, grinding sensations (crepitus), and joint thickening. In straightforward senior cases, an experienced vet can be fairly confident from the exam alone.

X-rays confirm it and rule out lookalikes. Radiographs show joint-fluid changes, cartilage loss (as narrowed joint space), and bone spurs. They also rule out the things you don't want to miss — bone tumors, fractures, or infections that can mimic arthritis pain. Typical cost: $150–$300 for the films, sometimes more with sedation for a painful or anxious dog.

Expect baseline bloodwork too, especially before starting long-term medication. It's not upselling — drugs like NSAIDs are processed by the liver and kidneys, and your vet needs to know those organs are healthy first. Senior blood panels typically run $100–$200.

Treatment options, from first-line to last-resort

Modern arthritis management is multimodal — several modest interventions stacked together usually beat one big one. Here's the menu roughly in the order vets reach for things:

1. Weight management (the highest-ROI treatment)

Every extra pound multiplies the load on already-damaged joints. Studies in Labradors showed lean dogs developed arthritis later and needed less medication. If your dog is overweight, a vet-supervised diet is genuinely more powerful than any supplement — and it's the cheapest intervention on this list. This is step zero, not an afterthought.

2. NSAIDs — the workhorse drugs

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatories (carprofen/Rimadyl, meloxicam, deracoxib) reduce joint inflammation and pain. Most arthritic dogs go on one daily, long-term, with periodic bloodwork to monitor liver and kidney values. They work well but aren't side-effect-free: watch for vomiting, diarrhea, appetite loss, or dark stools, and never combine NSAIDs or mix them with steroids without explicit vet direction.

3. Galliprant (grapiprant)

A newer daily pill that targets a specific pain receptor (EP4) rather than broadly blocking inflammation. It's often chosen for dogs that don't tolerate traditional NSAIDs well or need long-term therapy. It tends to cost more than generic NSAIDs.

4. Librela (bedinvetmab)

A monthly injection of an anti-nerve-growth-factor antibody, approved in the US in 2023. Instead of suppressing inflammation, it blocks a key pain-signaling molecule. Many owners report their dog "got its spark back" within weeks. It's given at the vet clinic, monthly, indefinitely — convenient, but it's one of the pricier ongoing options.

5. Adequan (polysulfated glycosaminoglycan)

A series of injections (typically twice weekly for a month, then tapered) that may help protect remaining cartilage and reduce joint inflammation. Often used alongside NSAIDs rather than instead of them.

6. Joint supplements

Glucosamine/chondroitin, omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA from fish oil), and newer options like undenatured type-II collagen or green-lipped mussel. Evidence is mixed but generally supportive as part of a plan, especially omega-3s. They're low-risk, modest-benefit — worth doing, not worth relying on alone. Look for products with published quality standards (e.g., NASC seal) since supplement regulation is light.

7. Physical rehabilitation

Underwater treadmill, therapeutic laser, targeted exercises, and sometimes acupuncture. Rehab builds the muscle that unloads damaged joints, and many dogs visibly improve within weeks. It's the closest thing to physical therapy for dogs — because it literally is.

8. Surgery

Reserved for severe, single-joint cases that don't respond to medical management: total hip replacement, or joint fusion (arthrodesis) for small joints. Effective but expensive and invasive — a last resort, not a first call.

Talk to your vet before starting anything. Dosing, drug interactions, and monitoring schedules are individual. This list is for understanding your options at the appointment — not for self-prescribing.

Home care checklist

What you do between vet visits matters as much as the prescriptions:

  • Orthopedic bed — thick memory foam, in a warm spot away from drafts. Thin beds on hard floors are the enemy.
  • Traction — non-slip rugs or runners on hardwood and tile, especially on the routes to food, water, and the door.
  • Ramps — for the car, the couch, and any porch steps. Every jump avoided is joint wear avoided.
  • Raised food and water bowls — less neck and shoulder strain for arthritic front legs.
  • Gentle, consistent exercise — short daily walks beat weekend marathons. Swimming is ideal if your dog likes water: full workout, zero impact.
  • Nail trims — overgrown nails change how the foot lands and stress joints up the leg. Keep them short.
  • Warmth — stiffness worsens in the cold. A dog sweater on winter walks isn't vanity; it's joint care.
  • Weight checks — weigh monthly. You can't manage what you don't measure, and "a little extra" creeps up fast in seniors.

When to call your vet

  • Sudden inability to stand or walk — this can be a neurological emergency, not "just arthritis."
  • A joint that is hot, markedly swollen, or suddenly much worse — possible infection or injury.
  • Vomiting, diarrhea, appetite loss, or dark/tarry stools while on NSAIDs — call the same day and pause the medication until advised.
  • Any medication that previously worked stops working — pain plans need retuning over time.

Frequently asked questions

Can arthritis in dogs be cured?

No — cartilage loss is irreversible. But it can be managed very successfully: most dogs on a consistent plan of weight control, pain medication, and joint support return to comfortable, active lives.

What is the best pain relief for dogs with arthritis?

Veterinarians usually start with an NSAID like carprofen or meloxicam, adding options like Galliprant or monthly Librela injections for dogs that need more help or don't tolerate NSAIDs. The "best" choice depends on your dog's health profile — it's a vet decision, not a product ranking.

Do joint supplements actually work for dogs?

Evidence is modest but real, particularly for omega-3 fatty acids. Think of supplements as supporting players: worthwhile alongside weight management and prescribed pain control, not replacements for them.

Should an arthritic dog still exercise?

Yes — controlled, low-impact exercise (leash walks, swimming) preserves the muscle that protects joints. What to avoid: sprinting, jumping, slippery floors, and the "weekend warrior" pattern of nothing-all-week then overdoing it.
